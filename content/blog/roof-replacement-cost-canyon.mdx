Roof replacement in Canyon runs about **$15,000 for a typical single-story home** as of April 2026, based on Xactimate-grade pricing for the Amarillo–Canyon market. That is roughly **$7.00 to $7.75 per square foot all-in** for a simple gable roof with architectural shingles. Two-story homes, steep pitches, and complex rooflines push the number up from there. These are insurance-grade figures — the kind of itemized scope your carrier works from — not a stripped-down retail cash bid.

## What Goes Into the Price

A roof replacement isn't one line item; it's a stack of them. On a typical 20-square job — about 2,000 sqft of roof surface — the scope includes tear-off and disposal, synthetic underlayment, a starter course, drip edge, valley metal, the shingles themselves, ridge cap, ridge vent, and flashing at every penetration.

A few Texas-specific notes shape that scope. Valley metal is standard practice in the Panhandle, typically adding a few hundred dollars on a cut-up roof. Ice-and-water barrier, by contrast, is not required by code in most Texas jurisdictions and is usually excluded from a standard scope. And because West Texas roof decks run hot, synthetic underlayment is strongly preferred over old felt.

## What Pushes the Number Higher

The $15,000 baseline is a simple, single-story gable. Real roofs add complexity, and complexity stacks:

- **Two-story home:** adds roughly 4 to 6 percent to the roof subtotal.
- **Steep pitch (7–9 in 12):** adds about 10 to 14 percent.
- **Very steep pitch (10–12 in 12):** adds 18 to 25 percent.
- **Cut-up roof** (multiple gables, hips, dormers): absorbed in a higher waste factor and more flashing.
- **Decking replacement:** about $2.25 to $3.50 per sqft of any rotted or delaminated area.
- **Class 4 impact-resistant upgrade:** about $85 to $125 per square.

As a real-world reference point, a two-story Amarillo-area home with a 7-to-9-in-12 pitch and a partial hip roof came in around **$17,820** on a 20-square scope — roughly an 18 to 19 percent premium over the simple-gable baseline. That is a useful rule of thumb for anything two-story and moderately steep.

## Insurance-Grade vs. Retail Cash Bids

One reason estimates vary so widely is that not all quotes describe the same work. Insurance-grade (Xactimate) scopes itemize everything — starter course, drip edge, valley metal, ventilation, flashing at every penetration — and apply overhead and profit on qualifying claims. Retail cash bids often come in lower because contractors bundle line items, skip itemized upgrades, and leave out overhead and profit. A rock-bottom number usually means a thinner scope, not a better deal. When you compare quotes, compare what is actually included, not just the bottom line.

## Insurance and Storm Damage

Because Canyon sits in hail country, a large share of replacements here run through insurance. If a storm damaged your roof, Texas law gives you two years from the date of loss to file. Under the [Texas Prompt Payment Act (Insurance Code Chapter 542)](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/IN/htm/IN.542.htm), your insurer must acknowledge the claim within 15 days and pay or deny it within 60 days. When you replace a storm-damaged roof, it is often the right time to upgrade to Class 4 shingles — [the Texas Department of Insurance notes](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/company/roofing-discounts.html) that carriers are required to offer premium credits for UL 2218 Class 4 impact-resistant materials.

## Canyon-Specific Considerations

Canyon is the seat of Randall County and part of the Amarillo metro, so pricing tracks the Panhandle baseline rather than the higher metro or North Texas hail-alley rates. Local pricing in West Texas runs within a point or two of the Amarillo figure. What does affect your number here is the weather history: repeated hail and hard freeze-thaw cycling age Panhandle roofs and frequently expose decking problems that only show up once the old shingles come off. That is why a real quote always follows an on-site measurement and a look at the deck — the listing square footage and a phone estimate can't account for what's underneath.
