The roof deck is the structural layer of wood sheathing — usually OSB or plywood — that's nailed directly to your roof trusses or rafters, sitting underneath the underlayment, shingles, and everything else you actually see from the ground. It's the foundation every other roofing component depends on: shingles are only as good as what they're nailed into, and a compromised deck undermines the whole system even if the shingles above it still look fine.

## What the Roof Deck Actually Does

The deck ties the roof structure together into one working surface, gives shingle nails something solid to bite into, and acts as the last line of defense if water ever gets past the underlayment. Most homes built in the last several decades use 7/16-inch to 5/8-inch OSB sheathing; older homes sometimes still have plank decking made of individual boards rather than sheets. Either way, shingle manufacturers require nailing into sound, dry sheathing to validate their wind and workmanship warranties — nail shingles into a soft or rotted deck and the fasteners won't hold, no matter how good the shingle itself is.

## Signs Your Roof Deck May Be Damaged

Deck problems usually show up before shingle problems do, if you know where to look:

- **Sagging or spongy spots** underfoot when a roofer walks the roof
- **Water stains or discoloration** on the attic ceiling or rafters
- **Visible daylight** coming through the sheathing when viewed from inside the attic
- **Musty smell** in the attic, often a sign of trapped moisture

Any of these usually means moisture has been getting past the shingles or underlayment for a while, and a full roof inspection is the only way to know how far it's spread.

## Roof Deck Considerations in the Texas Panhandle

Wind-driven rain during Panhandle storms tends to find its way through failed flashing or aged underlayment before it ever reaches the deck, so deck damage is often a sign of a problem that's been building for years rather than a single storm event. Because the region averages 14.3 mph winds and regularly sees severe hailstorms, a compromised deck under storm-battered shingles is a common finding during post-storm inspections — one more reason a full tear-off inspection matters more than a quick shingle-only patch after hail or wind damage.
