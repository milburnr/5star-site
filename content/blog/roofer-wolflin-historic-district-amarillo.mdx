## What Wolflin's Period Homes Need From a Roof

The brick homes around Wolflin Avenue and South Western tend to share three traits that shape any roofing job: steeper pitches, cut-up rooflines with dormers and multiple gables, and decades of prior repairs layered on top of each other.

Steep, complex roofs live or die on their flashing. Every valley, chimney, dormer, and sidewall is a transition where water wants to get in, and on an older home those details were often patched rather than rebuilt over the years. When we inspect a Wolflin roof, the flashing gets as much attention as the field shingles, because that's where Panhandle wind-driven rain finds its way inside.

## Matching New Shingles to an Older Home

The biggest worry Wolflin homeowners raise is that a new roof will make a historic home look like a subdivision build. It won't, if it's done thoughtfully. Today's architectural shingles carry dimensional shadow lines and come in weathered-wood, slate, and driftwood tones that read well against aged brick and painted trim. We bring physical samples and hold them against your actual exterior in daylight before anything is ordered, because a color that looks right in a brochure can look wrong on the wall.

Ventilation is the other half of the job. Many older Amarillo homes were built before balanced attic ventilation was standard, and a roof that can't breathe cooks its own shingles from below and voids the manufacturer warranty. Correcting intake and exhaust during a replacement is one of the highest-value upgrades on a period home.

Decking is the third thing we watch on Wolflin homes. Roofs built in the 1920s and 1930s often started with wood plank sheathing rather than modern plywood, and generations of re-roofs may have added or replaced boards unevenly. Once the old shingles come off, soft, split, or gapped decking has to be corrected before new shingles go down — you can't fasten a warranty-grade roof to a deck that won't hold nails. We'd rather find that on tear-off day and price it honestly than have it surface as a callback a year later.

## How to Vet Any Amarillo Roofer

Amarillo sees a wave of out-of-town crews after every big hail event. Protect yourself:

1. **Insist on a written, photo-backed scope of damage** before signing anything.
2. **Confirm the crew is local and licensed** — someone you can find again next year.
3. **Never sign over your claim** to a contractor who shows up uninvited after a storm.
4. **Get the inspection in writing** whether the outcome is a repair or a replacement.

The [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/storm-damage-tips.html) publishes clear guidance on avoiding storm-chasing scams, and it's worth a read before you hire anyone.

## Amarillo-Specific Considerations

Wolflin's tree canopy is part of its charm and part of its roofing challenge. Those mature elms shade the roof — which slows some UV wear — but they also drop limbs in the high winds that average 14.3 mph across Amarillo, and hail that fights through the canopy still bruises shingles below. Potter County has logged more than 131 hail days since 2000, including a 4.25-inch stone in May 2019, so even a shaded Wolflin roof earns a look after a serious storm. When replacement time comes, Class 4 impact-resistant shingles rated under UL Standard 2218 qualify for [insurance premium credits the state requires carriers to offer](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/impact-resistant-roofing.html) — a smart upgrade on a home you plan to keep.
