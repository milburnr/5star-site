At minimum, twice a year — once in spring and once in fall — plus an additional inspection after any major hailstorm, high-wind event, or heavy snow load. That's the standard maintenance rhythm recommended by the [National Roofing Contractors Association](https://www.nrca.net/education/custom-education/roof-repair-maintenance), and it's the schedule that keeps most commercial roof warranties valid. Property managers who wait for a leak to show up inside the building are almost always looking at a bigger repair bill than they would have with a scheduled inspection.

## Why twice a year isn't arbitrary

The spring and fall timing isn't just convention — it lines up with what actually stresses a low-slope commercial roof. A fall inspection catches summer sun damage and clears debris and drains before winter freeze-thaw cycles can force water under the membrane. A spring inspection catches whatever winter did — ice damming at drains, membrane contraction cracks, flashing pulled loose by wind — before summer heat and UV exposure make small problems worse.

The [IBHS Commercial Roofs Best Practices Guide](https://ibhs.org/wp-content/uploads/Commercial-Roofs-Best-Practices.pdf) frames it the same way: periodic inspection and maintenance exists to catch membrane punctures, debris buildup, and drainage failures before they become structural loading problems. Skipping a cycle doesn't just risk a leak — a roof carrying standing water it can't drain is carrying weight it wasn't designed for.

## What a real inspection checks

A scheduled inspection should cover more than a walk-around. Expect a commercial roofer to check:

- **Membrane condition** — seam separation, blistering, punctures, and UV degradation across the entire field, not just the areas visible from a ladder.
- **Drainage** — every roof drain, scupper, and gutter cleared, plus a check for ponding water that isn't draining within 48 hours.
- **Flashing and penetrations** — HVAC curbs, vent stacks, and parapet caps, which are where most commercial leaks actually start.
- **Fasteners and seams** — loose fasteners or lifted seams that wind can catch and peel back.
- **Written documentation** — dated photos and a written report, which is what a manufacturer or insurer will ask for later.

That last point matters more than it sounds. Skip the documentation and you don't just lose a paper trail — you may lose the warranty coverage that documentation was protecting.

Building age and roof system also change what an inspector looks for. Newer TPO and modified-bitumen roofs tend to fail at seams and penetrations first, while older built-up gravel roofs are more prone to ponding water and gravel displacement that exposes the membrane underneath. A generic checklist misses system-specific failure points — which is why it matters that whoever inspects your roof works on your type of system regularly, not just occasionally.

## Amarillo-specific considerations

Potter County sits inside what meteorologists call Hail Alley, where dry desert air meets Gulf moisture and produces some of the most frequent hailstorms in the country. Amarillo has logged 131 hail days since 2000, with 8-12 hailstorms in a typical year, and the largest recorded hailstone locally measured 4.25 inches — roughly softball-sized — in May 2019. A roof that looks fine from the ground can have hail bruising, granule loss, or membrane fatigue that only shows up on a close inspection.

A post-storm inspection with dated photos, filed promptly, is the difference between a claim that moves and one that stalls.
